민지|en|Hello and welcome back to English Weekly Radio. I'm Minji, your warm host for this week.
알렉스|en|And I'm Alex, your English coach. This is week 28 of 2026, and you had a busy week — five full lessons.
민지|en|Five lessons! You talked about car problems, different seasons, personality types, and even life values as you get older. Big topics.
알렉스|en|And today we're not going to just list the corrections. We want to understand why certain patterns keep showing up.
민지|en|When you understand the reason, the habit changes. That's our goal today. Let's go.
알렉스|en|Ready? Let's start.
민지|en|Our big theme this week — and this month — is articles. You know them: a, an, the. Three small words. Huge impact.
알렉스|en|Articles have shown up as a focus pattern seven times across 35 lessons. That is number one on the cumulative list.
민지|en|Alex, why is this one so hard to fix? We talk about it almost every week.
알렉스|en|Because Korean doesn't have articles. In Korean, you just say the noun. Context does the work. So a, an, the feel optional to Korean speakers.
민지|ko|한국어에는 관사가 없어요. 그냥 명사를 말하면 되죠. 그래서 영어의 a, an, the가 선택 사항처럼 느껴지는 거예요.
알렉스|en|But in English, articles are not optional. They carry information the listener needs.
민지|en|What kind of information?
알렉스|en|Think of it as a signal. "A car" means: I'm introducing something new — one of many cars. "The car" means: you already know which one I mean. We share that knowledge.
민지|en|Oh — like the car we were just talking about a minute ago.
알렉스|en|Exactly. "The" says: shared knowledge. "A" says: new item, first mention.
민지|en|Let's hear your real words from this week. You said: But if some electric car has some fire accident, it spread the other car and the building problem.
알렉스|en|The natural version: But if an electric car has a fire accident, it could spread to other cars and cause problems for the building.
민지|en|Notice "an electric car" — because an electric car fire is a new idea in this sentence. The listener doesn't have it in mind yet.
알렉스|en|And "a fire accident" — not "some fire accident." Some is for amounts of uncountable things, like some water or some time. For a single countable event, use a.
민지|ko|'some'은 셀 수 없는 양에 써요. 단수 셀 수 있는 사건에는 'a'가 맞아요. 그게 핵심입니다.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: Ask yourself — is this noun new to the listener? Yes: use a or an. Already mentioned or both of you know it? Use the.
민지|en|New to listener: a or an. Already known: the. Say that ten times in your head this week.
알렉스|en|Here's another one. You said: long time, during the long time, China don't make some great car.
민지|en|Better: For a long time, China didn't make great cars.
알렉스|en|Three fixes in one sentence. "During" takes a specific event, like during the meeting or during summer. For a span of time, use "for" — for a long time.
민지|en|And "China don't make" — the verb should match: China didn't make. Past tense.
알렉스|en|And "some great car" should just be "great cars" — plural, no article needed for a general statement about a whole category.
민지|en|General statements with plural nouns — no article. "Dogs are loyal." "Cars need fuel." "People make mistakes." No a, no the.
알렉스|ko|복수 명사로 일반적인 사실을 말할 때는 관사가 없어요. "Cars need fuel." 특정 차가 아니니까요.
민지|en|Now let's look at another pattern: verb collocation. Choosing the right verb.
알렉스|en|You said: In my case, I eat some medicine and eat the fruit enough.
민지|en|Natural version: I take some medicine and eat plenty of fruit.
알렉스|en|"Eat medicine" makes sense if you translate directly from Korean. 약을 먹다 — medicine goes in the mouth, so eat, right?
민지|ko|한국어로는 '약을 먹다'라고 하잖아요. 그런데 영어에서는 약은 먹는 게 아니라 take해요. 이게 콜로케이션이에요.
알렉스|en|In English, we take medicine. We take a pill, take a vitamin. The verb "take" is the partner of "medicine."
민지|en|Collocation means two words that always travel together. You learn them as a pair.
알렉스|en|Take medicine. Make a decision. Do exercise. Make a mistake. These are fixed partners in English.
민지|en|So when you learn a new noun, also learn: which verb does it travel with?
알렉스|en|And "eat the fruit enough" — the natural way is "eat plenty of fruit." Plenty means a lot, more than enough. It lives before the noun.
민지|en|Plenty of time, plenty of fruit, plenty of options. Nice word. Try using it today.
알렉스|en|Now — adjectives and adverbs. This week's new focus: Adjective vs Adverb and Noun Form.
민지|en|A quick rule: adjectives describe nouns. "A relaxed person." Adverbs describe verbs or adjectives. "She spoke clearly."
알렉스|en|You said: I have more relax, yeah, the emotion of emotion.
민지|en|Better: I'm more relaxed now.
알렉스|en|"Relax" is a verb. "Relaxed" is the adjective. So you say "I am relaxed" — not "I have relax."
민지|en|Think of it this way: you become something, so use the adjective form. I am tired. I am excited. I am relaxed.
알렉스|en|Alright, time for the Correction Clinic. Repeat after us if you can. Fast and focused.
민지|en|You said: I'm really have a stress.
알렉스|en|Better: I get really stressed.
민지|en|"I'm really have" mixes two sentence structures. Choose one: I am stressed. Or: I get stressed. Don't combine them.
알렉스|en|You said: my purpose is different season.
민지|en|Better: I have different preferences for each season.
알렉스|en|"Purpose" means goal or aim. But what you wanted to say was your preferences — what you enjoy. Different meaning, different word.
민지|en|You said: the rain is so long. If the one time long rainy, it is the one day or two days stay.
알렉스|en|Better: The rain lasts so long. If we have a long rainy spell, it can last for one or two days.
민지|en|Rain doesn't "be long" — it "lasts." Lasts means continues for a duration. The rain lasted three hours. It's been raining for three hours.
알렉스|en|And a "rainy spell" is a nice expression — a period of rainy weather. A cold spell, a dry spell, a rough spell. Spell means a stretch of time.
민지|en|You said: my character is their immediate friend.
알렉스|en|Better: I'm more of a spontaneous person.
민지|en|Immediate means right now or urgent. What you meant was spontaneous — doing things without planning. Acting on the moment.
알렉스|en|You said: I'm just an office worker, so it is not important to weather information.
민지|en|Better: As an office worker, weather information isn't critically important for my daily tasks.
알렉스|en|Two things: "not important to" — use "for" when you mean in the context of something. "Not important for my work." And "As an office worker" sets the scene more naturally than "I'm just."
민지|en|You said: our country people believed humility is so great in person and state.
알렉스|en|Better: People in our country used to believe that humility was a great virtue for individuals and the nation.
민지|en|Notice "used to believe" — this tells us it was true in the past but may have changed now. Used to is a powerful structure for past habits or past beliefs.
알렉스|en|You said: Because she was older.
민지|en|Better: Given her age.
알렉스|en|"Given her age" is a more condensed, natural way to say the same thing. "Given X" means considering X, or because of X. Very useful expression.
민지|en|Let's do a few more from this week. Quick fire. Repeat after me.
알렉스|en|You said: We have people in... your clothes that pull in your clothes, everything. Things because you have a cold season and these dreams have a hot and cold weather. So it is similar.
민지|en|Better: We have people who wear many layers because of the cold season, and we also have hot and cold weather. So it is similar.
알렉스|en|When you're speaking and lose the thread, slow down and restart the sentence cleanly. Starting over is not failure — it's smart speaking.
민지|en|You said: maybe one or two weeks this season is still, or rainy season in Korea.
알렉스|en|Better: The rainy season in Korea usually lasts for one or two more weeks.
민지|en|Notice "usually lasts" — give the sentence a verb that describes duration. Last, continue, go on. The rain goes on for two weeks.
알렉스|en|You said: We would prepare some, yeah, things to do.
민지|en|Better: We need to prepare some things.
알렉스|en|"Would" suggests a hypothetical — something conditional or imagined. If you mean a real plan, use "need to" or "have to." We need to prepare. Simpler and clearer.
민지|en|You said: the young generation leads to a balance of their values in our life.
알렉스|en|Better: The young generation seeks a balance in their values.
민지|en|"Leads to" means causes something — it rains, which leads to flooding. But here the meaning is actively looking for something. That's "seek." They seek balance, not cause it.
알렉스|en|Seek, look for, search for — three ways to say you want something and are going after it. The young generation seeks balance. Try that one.
민지|en|Now, Vocabulary in Context. Not definitions — words in action.
알렉스|en|Word one: humid. Sentence: "Seoul in July is so humid that even the air feels wet." Humid means hot and full of moisture. Not just warm — wet heat.
민지|en|Related word: humidity. "The humidity hits you the moment you step outside." And the opposite: dry. Los Angeles is hot but dry. Very different feeling.
알렉스|en|Word two: balmy. "It was a balmy evening — perfect for sitting outside." Balmy means pleasantly warm, mild. A step up from nice weather.
민지|en|A balmy breeze. A balmy spring morning. It makes you sound a little poetic.
알렉스|en|Topic Talk — personality words from this week's discussions. These are A2 to B1 level, very useful in real life.
민지|en|Spontaneous. "She's really spontaneous — she booked a trip to Busan this morning with no plan." Acting on the moment.
알렉스|en|Laid-back. "He's pretty laid-back. He never worries about small things." Relaxed, easy-going, calm.
민지|en|Assertive. "She's assertive in meetings. She always speaks her mind clearly." Confident and direct — in a good way.
알렉스|en|Composure. "He kept his composure even when the meeting went badly." Staying calm under pressure. Composure is something you keep or lose.
민지|en|And prioritize. "As I get older, I prioritize time with family over long work hours." To choose what matters most first.
알렉스|en|Spontaneous, laid-back, assertive, composure, prioritize. Try using one of these today in a real sentence.
민지|en|Now — Culture Corner. Something surprising and a little fun.
알렉스|en|This week's story: why do English speakers say "break a leg" to wish someone good luck?
민지|en|Wait — break a leg? That sounds terrible, not lucky.
알렉스|en|I know! It's a famous theater expression. Before going on stage, actors say "break a leg" to each other instead of "good luck."
민지|en|Why not just say good luck?
알렉스|en|Superstition. Theater people believed that saying "good luck" out loud would bring bad luck. So they started saying the opposite.
민지|ko|영어 배우들은 직접 '행운을 빌어'라고 하면 오히려 불운이 온다고 믿었어요. 그래서 반대로 말하기 시작한 거예요.
알렉스|en|Now "break a leg" is used for any big moment — a job interview, a presentation, a first date. "You've got a big meeting? Break a leg!"
민지|en|And here's the connection to Korean. "화이팅" doesn't translate into English. If you say "fighting!" to cheer on an English speaker, they'll look confused.
알렉스|en|Instead, say: "You've got this." Or "Go for it." Or simply "Good luck." These are the English equivalents.
민지|ko|영어로 응원할 때는 '화이팅' 대신 'You've got this!' 또는 'Go for it!'를 쓰세요. 'fighting'은 영어권에서는 응원 표현이 아니에요.
알렉스|en|Language is cultural. Some expressions just don't cross the border.
민지|en|Here's one more fun one. In English, if you want to say something is very good, you might say "That's sick." Or "That's fire." These are slang — and they mean the opposite of what they sound like to a non-native speaker.
알렉스|en|So context matters a lot. A native speaker will always tell you the meaning from tone and situation. When in doubt, ask — native speakers love explaining their own slang.
민지|ko|'sick'이나 'fire'가 칭찬이라는 걸 처음엔 절대 모르죠. 맥락으로 배우는 게 살아있는 영어 공부예요.

알렉스|en|Articles: new noun, first mention — use a or an. Already known — use the. General plural statement — no article.
민지|en|Verb collocation: medicine takes take. Learn the verb that travels with the noun.
