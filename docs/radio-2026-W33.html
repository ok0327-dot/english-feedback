알렉스|en|And I'm Alex, your English coach. This is Week 33 of 2026, and what a week it was.
민지|en|Five lessons this week. Topics: family traditions, love at first sight, travel planning, packing strategies, and travel styles.
알렉스|en|Big conversations, rich material. And today we are not just listing what went wrong. We are going to understand it.
민지|en|Because when you understand why a pattern keeps showing up, you can fix it — not just avoid it today, but fix it for good.
알렉스|en|Exactly. Okay Minji, let's start with the headline. What was the number one grammar pattern this week?
민지|en|Subject-Verb Agreement. Nine times in our cumulative record. Nine. That is the most of any grammar category so far.
알렉스|en|And it showed up again this week. So let's really dig in. Minji, what's the Korean connection here?
민지|en|In Korean, the verb does not change based on who is doing the action. "가다" is "가다" whether it's I, you, he, or they.
알렉스|en|But in English, there is one very specific rule. Third person singular — he, she, or it — gets a special verb form.
민지|ko|한국어에선 주어가 뭐든 동사가 바뀌지 않아요. 영어는 '그, 그녀, 그것' 단수 주어일 때 동사에 S를 붙여야 해요.
알렉스|en|He goes. She walks. It works. That S is a signal to the listener — exactly one thing is doing this action.
민지|en|Now, the trickier part that showed up this week. What happens when you connect two subjects with "and"?
알렉스|en|"My son and my wife." Two people. Connected by "and." That makes a compound subject — and compound means plural.
민지|en|This week you said: "my son and my wife has many clothes and many belongings."
알렉스|en|The natural fix: "my son and my wife have a lot of clothes and belongings." Have, not has. Two people equals plural verb.
민지|ko|'A and B' — and로 연결된 두 주어는 항상 복수예요. 'has'가 아니라 'have'. 예외가 없는 규칙이에요.
알렉스|en|The rule of thumb, and say this out loud: A and B always takes a plural verb. A and B have. A and B are. A and B do.
민지|en|Let's practice. Repeat after us. "My son and my wife have a big suitcase."
알렉스|en|"Alex and Minji are your hosts." "The book and the pen are on the table."
민지|en|One more. "My mom and my dad have been married for thirty-five years."
알렉스|en|Every time you join two nouns with "and" — pause for one second. Ask: is this one or more than one? More than one — use the plural form.
민지|en|Here's a quick twist. "Everyone" sounds like it includes many people, right? But it's actually singular in grammar.
알렉스|en|"Everyone is here." Not "everyone are here." Same for somebody, nobody, anything — grammatically one thing, always singular.
민지|en|So to be clear: "A and B" — two things joined by and — always plural. "Everyone, someone, nobody" — always singular. Keep those two rules separate.
민지|en|Now let's talk about Articles quickly. Number two in our cumulative list. Eight instances.
알렉스|en|A, an, the. Three tiny words with enormous impact on how natural you sound.
민지|en|The key question to ask yourself before every noun: does my listener already know about this specific thing?
알렉스|en|First time mentioning it — use "a." Already shared knowledge between you and your listener — use "the."
민지|ko|처음 언급할 땐 'a', 이미 공유된 대상엔 'the'. 핵심 질문 하나: 내 청자도 이 대상을 알고 있나? 이것만 기억하면 돼요.
민지|en|Example: "I saw a dog today. The dog was wearing a tiny hat." First mention: a dog. Second mention: the same dog — so the dog.
알렉스|en|Train yourself to ask that question before every noun. It becomes automatic faster than you think.
민지|en|Now our second deep-dive pattern: Countable and Uncountable Nouns. This one is genuinely hard for Korean speakers.
알렉스|en|Because in Korean, this distinction barely exists in the grammar. "물 많다" — lots of water. "가방 많다" — lots of bags. The nouns look the same.
민지|ko|한국어는 가산·불가산 명사를 문법으로 구분하지 않아요. 영어는 이 구분이 동사와 관사 선택에 직접 영향을 줘요.
알렉스|en|In English, bags is countable. One bag, two bags, many bags — it has a plural form.
민지|en|But water is uncountable. It has no plural. You cannot say "two waters" or "many informations." Just water. Just information.
알렉스|en|This week you said: "I had so sweat on my body." Sweat as a substance — that's uncountable. You can't say "a sweat" in that sense.
민지|en|Natural fix: "I would sweat a lot." Or "I was sweating a lot." Turn the noun into a verb — the problem disappears.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: if you can't put "a" directly in front of it — a sweat, a water, an information — it is probably uncountable.
민지|en|And to count uncountable nouns, use a unit word. A glass of water. A drop of sweat. A piece of information. Beautiful and correct.
알렉스|en|A few more uncountables that often trip people up: advice, luggage, furniture, equipment.
민지|en|"I have some advice for you." Not "some advices." "The luggage is heavy." Not "the luggages." One form for all amounts.
알렉스|en|Travel word to remember: baggage and luggage are both uncountable. You check your luggage — not your luggages.
민지|en|Alright. Let's move to the Correction Clinic. Fifteen corrections this week. Quick-fire — repeat after us if you can.
알렉스|en|You said: "the temperature is getting a little lower, going low."
민지|en|Better: "the temperature is starting to cool down a bit." Cool down is the natural phrasal verb for temperature dropping.
알렉스|en|You said: "it's so special for me. It's so nice."
민지|en|Better: "It felt very special to me." Past tense — felt. That moment has passed. And "special to me" is the natural phrase.
알렉스|en|You said: "So if I want, I will go to a different beach."
민지|en|Better: "So I can visit a different beach whenever I want." Whenever is more natural than "if I want." And can instead of will.
알렉스|en|You said: "And you have a happy Monday night."
민지|en|Better: "I hope you had a happy Monday night." English wishes need "I hope." And the night is past — so use had.
알렉스|en|You said: "Where is popular for a first date in your country?"
민지|en|Better: "What is a popular place for a first date in your country?" Where asks for a location. What asks for a thing. A place is a thing — use what.
알렉스|ko|'어디가 유명해요?'를 직역하면 'where is popular?'가 되지만, 영어는 'what is a popular place?'라고 물어요. 장소 이름을 원할 땐 'what'이에요.
민지|en|You said: "talking something."
알렉스|en|Better: "talking about something." Talk always needs a preposition. Talk about, talk to, talk with. Never just "talk something."
민지|en|You said: "my printed paper is really removed some document."
알렉스|en|Better: "Some parts of my printed paper are missing." Removed suggests someone took it. Missing just means it's not there — natural and simple.
민지|en|You said: "I love to Middle Asia airline."
알렉스|en|Better: "I love Middle Eastern airlines." The region is the Middle East — adjective form is Middle Eastern. And airlines is plural here, used as a category.
민지|en|You said: "Almost I've used the same backpack for ten years."
알렉스|en|Better: "I've used the same backpack for almost ten years." Almost modifies ten years — so it goes right before the number, not at the start of the sentence.
민지|en|You said: "I'm prepared for next week in China."
알렉스|en|Better: "I'm getting ready for my trip to China next week." Getting ready is natural and active. Prepared sounds formal. And: trip to China, not just "for China."
민지|en|You said: "I'm not having any technical issues today."
알렉스|en|Better: "I'm not experiencing any technical issues today." We experience situations — not have them. Experience is the more natural word here.
민지|en|Last one. You said: "So if they are go travel to abroad, they are travel like a work."
알렉스|en|Better: "So when they travel abroad, they often treat it like work." Two fixes: travel abroad needs no "to." And treat it like work — not "travel like a work."
민지|en|Clinic complete. Solid work getting through all fifteen.
알렉스|en|Before we move on — one pattern from the clinic is worth pausing on. "Talking something" versus "talking about something."
민지|en|This one comes up all the time. Talk, think, dream, worry — these verbs in English always need a preposition before the topic.
알렉스|en|Talk about it. Think about it. Dream about it. Worry about it. The preposition is not optional — it's part of the verb itself.
민지|en|And "travel abroad" — no "to." Because abroad already contains the direction. Saying "travel to abroad" is like saying "go to outside." It's built in.
알렉스|en|Two preposition fixes to take into next week: talk ABOUT, travel ABROAD. Say them ten times today. Make them automatic.

알렉스|en|This week's theme was travel. Let's bring the vocabulary to life with real sentences, not just definitions.
민지|en|Heirloom. "This ring is a family heirloom — my grandmother wore it every single day for forty years."
알렉스|en|Tight-knit. "We're a tight-knit family. Even when we disagree, we always come back to each other."

알렉스|en|Layover. "My layover in Istanbul was eight hours, so I left the airport and had dinner in the old city."
민지|en|Off-season. "Off-season travel is underrated. Cheaper flights, smaller crowds, and locals actually have time to talk to you."
알렉스|en|Now Topic Talk — five travel expressions at A2 to B1 level that native speakers use all the time.
민지|en|One: get away. "I really need to get away this weekend. Even one night somewhere different resets everything."
알렉스|en|Two: tourist trap. "That seafood place near the pier is a total tourist trap — double the price, half the quality."
민지|en|Three: local gem. "Our guesthouse owner told us about a local gem — a tiny noodle shop with no English menu. Best food of the whole trip."
알렉스|en|Four: change of scenery. "I didn't go far — just a different neighborhood — but the change of scenery did wonders for my mood."
민지|en|Five: pack light. "I've learned to pack light. One carry-on bag for two weeks. If I forget something, I buy it there."
알렉스|en|Collocations to remember: pack light, get away, tourist trap, local gem, change of scenery. Natural English is built on combinations like these.
민지|en|And remember — learning the combination is not the same as learning the word alone. Pack light, not pack lightly. Tourist trap, not tourist pitfall.
알렉스|en|Textbook English gives you individual words. Real English gives you the combinations. Prioritize combinations every time.
민지|en|Use one of those today. Say it out loud. That's the moment it stops being a note and starts becoming your English.
알렉스|en|Now — Culture Corner. Did You Know? My favorite part of the show.

알렉스|en|The word "deadline." You hear it everywhere — at work, at school, in conversation. But where did it come from?
민지|en|It sounds dramatic, right? Dead. Line. Like something terrible happens if you cross it.
알렉스|en|Something very terrible, actually. This expression comes from American Civil War prison camps in the 1860s.
민지|en|There was a physical line drawn around the prison — sometimes a wall, sometimes just a mark on the ground.
알렉스|en|Any prisoner who crossed that line would be shot immediately. No warning. That was the "dead line." Cross it and you are dead.
민지|ko|1860년대 미국 남북전쟁 포로수용소에서 '이 선을 넘으면 즉시 사살'이라는 실제 경계선이 'dead line'이었어요. 그게 지금의 '마감 기한'이 된 거예요.
알렉스|en|Later, newspaper publishers borrowed the idea. If text went past a certain mark on the printing plate, it couldn't be printed. That mark — also called the deadline.
민지|en|And from there it spread to mean any final limit. Any point of no return. After this moment, it is too late.
알렉스|en|Language carries history in its bones. Every word has traveled through time to reach you — that's what makes it worth learning.
민지|en|Next time your work deadline feels stressful — remember, at least nobody gets shot for missing it anymore.
