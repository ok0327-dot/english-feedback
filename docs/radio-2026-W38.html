알렉스|en|Because when you understand the reason behind a mistake, the correction actually sticks.
민지|en|Think of it this way. You do not just want to fix one sentence. You want to fix the pattern.
알렉스|en|Let us start with a question. Why does the same mistake keep coming back even after you fix it?
민지|en|Because the root of the mistake is still there. Your Korean brain is still in charge of your English mouth.
알렉스|en|That is completely normal. Every language learner goes through this phase. The solution is awareness.
알렉스|en|And fixing a pattern means understanding the root. So let us dig in.
민지|en|Section One: Why It Happens. Two patterns this week that need your attention.
알렉스|en|Pattern One: the -ed versus -ing adjectives. Tired versus tiring.
민지|en|This week you said, "It is a tired Monday because I had ice swimming with my son."
알렉스|en|Beautiful detail about your life. But "tired Monday" is the problem.
민지|en|The natural version is: "It is a tiring Monday." Let me explain why.
알렉스|en|Here is the rule. When a word ends in -ED, it describes how YOU feel inside.
민지|en|Tired. Bored. Excited. Confused. Disappointed. These describe the person who receives the feeling.
알렉스|en|When a word ends in -ING, it describes what CAUSES that feeling in you.
민지|en|Tiring. Boring. Exciting. Confusing. Disappointing. These describe the thing that creates the feeling.
알렉스|ko|한국어는 '피곤한 월요일'처럼 능동과 수동을 구분 안 해요. 영어는 달라요. -ed는 내가 받는 감정, -ing는 남에게 주는 감정이에요.
민지|en|Monday does not feel tired. Monday MAKES you feel tired. So Monday is tiring.
알렉스|en|Let me give you some pairs so you can feel the difference. A terrifying movie — you are terrified.
민지|en|A fascinating lecture — you are fascinated. A confusing rule — you are confused.
알렉스|en|Your quick trick: ask yourself, is this the cause or the receiver? The cause gets -ING. The receiver gets -ED.
민지|en|Practice out loud: "The long meeting was..." tiring! And after the meeting, you felt? Tired. 
알렉스|en|Try one more: "The news was shocking." You were? Shocked. Yes, that is the pattern.
민지|en|Pattern Two: uncountable nouns. This week you said, "I had three luggages."
알렉스|en|In English, luggage is uncountable. Like water, like furniture, like advice, like information.
민지|en|You cannot put a number directly in front of these words.
알렉스|en|Instead, use "pieces of." Three pieces of luggage. Two pieces of furniture. A piece of advice. One piece of information.
민지|ko|한국어는 짐 하나 둘처럼 셀 수 있지만, 영어 luggage는 물처럼 셀 수 없는 명사예요. pieces of를 쓰세요.
알렉스|en|Your rule of thumb: luggage is like water. You cannot say "three waters." So do not say "three luggages."
민지|en|Other uncountable nouns to watch: news, progress, equipment, homework, traffic, feedback, and knowledge.
알렉스|en|You never say "many informations" or "several advices." You say "a lot of information" or "some advice."
민지|en|Practice this sentence now: "I checked two pieces of luggage at the airport." Say it three times.
알렉스|en|Good. Now let us go to Section Two: Correction Clinic. Quick-fire practice. Repeat after us!
민지|en|You said: Is it more going down?
알렉스|en|Natural: Is it getting colder?
민지|en|English uses "getting" to show change over time. Getting colder. Getting better. Getting late. Getting worse.
알렉스|en|You said: I always saw the Terracotta about history book.
민지|en|Natural: I always read about the Terracotta Warriors in history books.
알렉스|en|Two fixes: "read about" — you read books, you do not see them. And "history books" goes plural here.
민지|en|You said: Why that expression used the element?
알렉스|en|Natural: Why is that expression used with the word element?
민지|en|English questions need the verb near the front. "Why IS that expression used?" The IS comes early.
알렉스|en|You said: I make a plan to travel, I am just mixed, relaxation and adventure.
민지|en|Natural: If I plan a trip, I like to mix relaxation and adventure.
알렉스|en|"Plan a trip" is shorter and more natural than "make a plan to travel." And "mix" is the right verb for combining two things.
민지|en|You said: I think this sentence meaning is a change in environment.
알렉스|en|Natural: I think this phrase means a change in environment.
민지|en|Notice "phrase" instead of "sentence meaning." And "means" with an S in third person — it means, not meaning.
알렉스|en|You said: Before I try it, I expected it is to be melty.
민지|en|Natural: Before I tried it, I expected it to be melty.
알렉스|en|"Expected" is past tense, so everything goes past. And the structure is "expected it TO BE" — infinitive.
민지|en|You said: After work, I will go to some dinner with my friends.
알렉스|en|Natural: After work, I will have dinner with my friends.
민지|en|You "have" meals in English. Have breakfast. Have lunch. Have dinner. "Go to dinner" usually means going out to a restaurant.
알렉스|en|You said: Everything is perfect for the weather. We have a vivid color and tree.
민지|en|Natural: Everything is perfect for this weather. We have vivid colors and trees.
알렉스|en|Two small fixes: use "this weather" for a specific current weather, and vivid colors plus trees both go plural.
알렉스|en|You said: because they select so interesting section and made some hype advertisement.
민지|en|Natural: because they select such interesting sections and create hyped advertisements.
알렉스|en|"Such interesting" before a noun, not "so interesting." And sections and advertisements go plural.
민지|en|You said: I finally tried the Dubai cookie after seeing it blow up.
알렉스|en|Natural: after seeing it become so popular.
민지|en|"Blow up" can sound negative in some contexts. "Become so popular" is always clear and positive.
알렉스|en|Section Three: Vocabulary in Context. Words should live in real sentences.
민지|en|First word: ultimate. The highest level, the absolute best.
알렉스|en|Sentence: "Kyoto is on my ultimate travel bucket list." Or: "That meal was the ultimate comfort food."
민지|en|Second: itchy feet. An idiom for a strong desire to travel and see new places.
알렉스|en|Sentence: "After two weeks at home, I am getting itchy feet." You feel restless and want to go somewhere.
민지|en|Third: hype. Excitement or attention — sometimes more than something deserves.
알렉스|en|"The Dubai chocolate got so much hype on social media." And then: was it worth the hype? Was it really that good?
민지|en|Fourth: cliffhanger. A dramatic ending that leaves you desperate to know what comes next.
알렉스|en|Sentence: "That drama ended on such a cliffhanger — I had to watch the next episode at midnight."
민지|en|Fifth: bougie. Informal slang for fancy, expensive, or acting upper-class.
알렉스|en|Sentence: "That cafe is a bit too bougie for me — their smallest coffee costs fifteen dollars."
민지|en|Bonus word this week: decadent. Very rich, indulgent, or luxurious — especially for food.
알렉스|en|Sentence: "That dark chocolate cake was absolutely decadent." Great word for reviewing food.
민지|en|Now, Topic Talk. Five food expressions for real daily conversations at A2 to B1 level.
알렉스|en|Number one: hit the spot. Something is exactly what you needed at that moment.
민지|en|Example: "This warm soup really hits the spot on a cold autumn day."
알렉스|en|Number two: comfort food. Food that makes you feel warm, happy, or nostalgic.
민지|en|Example: "Doenjang jjigae is the ultimate Korean comfort food for me."
알렉스|en|Number three: foodie. A person who loves food, cooking, and eating.
민지|en|Example: "I am a total foodie — I always research restaurants before I travel."
알렉스|en|Number four: worth the hype. The popular thing is actually as good as people say.
민지|en|Example: "I tried the Dubai chocolate. Honestly? It was totally worth the hype."
알렉스|en|Number five: crowd-pleaser. Something almost everyone enjoys.
민지|en|Example: "Pizza is always a crowd-pleaser at office parties. Nobody argues about pizza."
알렉스|en|Section Four: Culture Corner. Today — a story about a word you used this week.
민지|en|The word is: cliffhanger. You used it when talking about TV shows with open or surprising endings.
알렉스|en|In the 1800s in England and America, stories were printed in weekly newspapers — one chapter at a time.
민지|en|Writers wanted readers to buy the next issue. So they ended each chapter at the most dramatic possible moment.
알렉스|en|Sometimes a hero was literally hanging from a cliff. And the chapter ended right there. Will they survive? Find out next week!
민지|en|It was a marketing trick and a brilliant storytelling technique. Readers were desperate for the next issue.
알렉스|ko|'cliffhanger'는 1800년대 신문 연재소설에서 왔어요. 절벽에 매달린 장면에서 끊어 독자가 다음 주 신문을 사게 만들었죠.
민지|en|Today we use "cliffhanger" for TV dramas, movies, sports matches — any moment that ends without a clear conclusion.
알렉스|en|So the next time your favorite drama ends on a cliffhanger, blame the Victorian newspaper writers.
민지|en|And here is a quick bonus. The word bougie that you used this week comes from the French word bourgeois.
알렉스|en|Bourgeois meant the educated middle class in France — merchants, professionals, city people.
민지|en|Over time in American English, especially in slang, it became bougie — meaning acting fancy or too expensive.
알렉스|ko|'bougie'는 프랑스어 bourgeois에서 왔어요. 중산층을 뜻했는데 지금은 고급인 척한다는 슬랭이 됐죠.
민지|en|Language carries history. Every word you use today has a story behind it.
