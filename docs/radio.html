민지|en|These two changes will have the biggest impact on how natural you sound. Let's start with Articles.
알렉스|en|Here's what you said: "It's a Monday and it's going into autumn season, so it's a little chilly."
민지|en|The natural version: "It's Monday, and as autumn approaches, it's a little chilly."

민지|ko|한국어엔 관사가 없어서 영어의 a, an, the 선택이 항상 낯설게 느껴지죠. 이게 한국 학습자들이 공통으로 겪는 어려움이에요.
알렉스|en|In English, days of the week — Monday, Tuesday, all of them — don't use "a" in general statements. You say "It's Monday" or "I'll see you on Monday."
민지|en|Think of it this way. "A Monday" sounds like you're picking one Monday out of many possible Mondays. That's strange.
알렉스|en|Now the flip side. You said "Canada and USA." The natural version is "Canada and the USA."
민지|en|Why does "the USA" need "the"? Countries named as a group or union — the USA, the UK, the Netherlands — always take "the." It's a fixed rule.
알렉스|en|One more example from this week. You said "the passcode" when introducing it for the first time. Native speakers say "a passcode" there.
민지|en|Here is the core rule, and it's worth repeating slowly.
알렉스|en|First mention — use "a" or "an." New information, new to the listener. "I set up a passcode."
민지|en|Second mention, or when both speakers already know which one — use "the." "The passcode is on your phone."
알렉스|ko|처음 나오는 정보엔 a, 듣는 사람도 아는 정보엔 the. 청자의 머릿속에 있느냐 없느냐가 기준이에요.
알렉스|en|Think of "a" as introducing, and "the" as pointing. "I bought a book. The book was amazing."
민지|en|Rule of thumb: before you say the noun, ask yourself — does my listener already know which one? If yes, say "the." If no, say "a."
알렉스|en|Now let's move to our second deep dive — Verb Tense for near-future plans.
민지|en|Here's what you said: "Today, we have a dinner with my coworker."
알렉스|en|The natural version: "Today, I'm having dinner with my coworker."
민지|en|Two things changed. "We" became "I" — you're talking about your own plan. And "have a dinner" became "I'm having dinner."
알렉스|en|Why "I'm having" and not "I have dinner"? This is one of the most important tense choices in spoken English.
민지|en|When a plan is already arranged — you booked the restaurant, or your coworker said "let's meet at seven" — English uses the present continuous.
알렉스|ko|이미 약속이 잡힌 가까운 미래 계획엔 현재진행형을 써요. "I'm meeting" "I'm going" "I'm having" 이런 식으로요.
민지|en|It's like the plan is already in motion, already happening in your mind.
알렉스|en|Compare these two: "I have a meeting tomorrow" — that sounds like a general fact. "I'm having a meeting tomorrow" — that sounds like it's already scheduled, already real.
민지|en|More examples: "I'm flying to Jeju next week." — already booked. "I'm meeting my client on Thursday." — already confirmed.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: if the plan is already made — time, place, people — use I'm plus verb-ing.
민지|en|This is a very English way of thinking. Korean uses the same verb form for all near-future situations. English separates "arranged future" from simple facts.

알렉스|en|Better: "That's what I mean." This phrase — "that's what I mean" — is one of the most useful in everyday English conversation.
