민지|en|Hello and welcome back to English Weekly Radio! I'm Minji, your host for this Friday session.
알렉스|en|And I'm Alex, your English coach. We are in 2026 Week 45. Five lessons this week — health insurance, ID documents, taxes, and the post office. Big week, Joey!
민지|en|Five very different topics. That takes real courage to show up every day and tackle new vocabulary.
알렉스|en|And today, our goal is not just listing mistakes. We're going to understand WHY they happen. That's how you stop them.
민지|en|Let's start with the big picture. Alex, what was the main pattern this week?
알렉스|en|Two patterns dominated: articles — a, an, the — and subject-verb agreement. And here's the interesting thing — both come from the same root cause.
민지|en|Same root? Tell us!
알렉스|en|Both happen because Korean and English handle information differently. Korean uses word order and particles. English uses small grammar words like a, an, the, and verb endings.
민지|ko|한국어는 순서와 조사로 정보를 전달하지만, 영어는 a, an, the 같은 작은 단어와 동사 변화로 전달해요. 그래서 한국어 사고 그대로 영어를 하면 이 두 패턴에서 자주 걸려요.
알렉스|en|Right. So let's talk articles first. This is the number one cumulative weakness — 19 times so far.
민지|en|Nineteen times! Alex, why do Korean speakers struggle with this so much?
알렉스|en|Simple reason: Korean has zero articles. Zero. In Korean, you say the equivalent of "I have interview today" and everyone understands from context.
민지|en|But in English, "I have interview today" sounds incomplete. The listener thinks — which interview? One? Several?
알렉스|en|Exactly. English listeners need a small signal. A or an says: this is new information. The says: we both already know which one.
민지|ko|a 또는 an은 "이건 새로운 정보예요"라는 신호이고, the는 "우리 둘 다 아는 바로 그것"이라는 신호예요. 이 두 역할을 기억하면 관사의 70%가 해결됩니다.
알렉스|en|Let me tell you a quick story to make it stick. Ready?
민지|en|Ready!
알렉스|en|I saw a dog in the park today. The dog was huge. It had a ball in its mouth. The ball was bright red.
민지|en|A dog — first time we hear about it. The dog — now we both know which dog. A ball — new information. The ball — that specific ball.
알렉스|en|That's the entire article system in four sentences. First mention gets a or an. Second mention gets the.
민지|en|Rule of thumb: First time you mention something? Use a or an. Both of you already know it? Use the.
알렉스|en|Now let's hear this from your real speech. You said: I have to interview at dinner time, so I need to change my schedule.
민지|en|Natural version: I have an interview scheduled for dinner time, so I need to change my schedule.
알렉스|en|An interview — because this is the first time the listener hears about it. It's new information. Brand new. So we use an.
민지|en|And in professional English, skipping the article sounds unfinished. "I have interview" feels like a word is missing.
알렉스|en|Here's another article correction. You asked your tutor: Do you have an experience to take an ambulance or a helicopter?
민지|en|Natural: Have you ever taken an ambulance or a helicopter?
알렉스|en|Two things changed. First, we don't say "have an experience" for general life events. We say "have you ever done" something.
민지|en|And second, "take" becomes "taken" because we use the present perfect here — have you ever TAKEN.
민지|ko|경험을 물어볼 때는 "Have you ever 과거분사" 형태를 써요. Have you ever taken, have you ever been, have you ever seen 처럼요.
알렉스|en|Now let's shift to pattern number two: subject-verb agreement. Sixteen times in total.
민지|en|This one hit again this week. Tell us why it happens.
알렉스|en|In Korean, verbs stay the same no matter who does the action. "나는 있다, 그는 있다, 그들은 있다" — the verb never changes form.
민지|en|But in English, the verb shifts. I have, but she has. They are, but it is. The verb tracks the subject.
알렉스|en|This week, you said: Every country have a different policy. What's the fix?
민지|en|Every country HAS a different policy. Because "every" in English always takes a singular verb.
알렉스|en|Even though "every country" feels like many countries, in English "every" means you're counting them one by one — separately. So it's singular.
민지|ko|"every"는 많은 것을 가리키는 것 같아 보여도 영어에서는 단수예요. "Every student has a book" — 학생 한 명 한 명이 각각 책을 갖고 있다는 뜻이에요.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: Every plus any noun? Always use has, is, was. Every plan includes. Every country needs. Every student has.
민지|en|And one more from this week: It hard to understand it clearly. What's missing?
알렉스|en|The be verb! It IS hard to understand it clearly. In English, you cannot remove is or are. Subject plus verb — always.
민지|en|In Korean, you can say "이해하기 어렵다" and the sentence is complete. No extra linking word needed.
알렉스|en|But English has a strict rule: subject and verb are always a pair. It is, it was, it will be. The be verb is always there.
민지|en|Alright, let's lock in those two rules. First mention of a noun needs a or an. Every plus noun takes a singular verb. And never drop the be verb.
알렉스|en|Now, Correction Clinic time! Quick-fire round. Try repeating each one after us.
민지|en|You said: I'm doing traveling.
알렉스|en|Natural: I'm traveling. Travel is already a verb. You never need "doing" in front of it. I'm traveling. I'm working. I'm cooking. Each verb stands alone.
민지|en|You said: After your class, finished your class, I moved to the city, to Ulsan.
알렉스|en|Natural: After your class, I'm heading to Ulsan. Use "heading to" for a trip that hasn't happened yet.
민지|en|Also — "I moved to Ulsan" sounds like you already arrived. For a future trip, say heading to or making my way to.
알렉스|en|You said: I came back to my office and finished my work travel. I came back to normal days.
민지|en|Natural: I'm back in the office after my work trip, and I'm glad to be back to my usual routine.
알렉스|en|Two notes there. First: "work trip," not "work travel." A trip is a specific journey. Travel is more general. Second: "usual routine," not "normal days" — routine is the natural word here.
민지|en|You said: My son doesn't have a residency proof, so usually use the passport in local or abroad.
알렉스|en|Natural: My son doesn't have proof of residency, so he usually uses his passport both locally and abroad.
민지|en|Notice: "proof of residency" — not "residency proof." In English, we often say the describing word AFTER "of." Proof of residency. Certificate of achievement. Letter of recommendation.
알렉스|en|And "locally and abroad," not "in local or abroad." Locally is an adverb — no need for "in."
민지|en|You said: If we have an interview after work, at that time it is kept secret to the investor.
알렉스|en|Natural: If we conduct an interview after work, it's kept confidential from investors.
민지|en|Two upgrades: "conduct an interview" sounds more professional than "have an interview" in a business setting. And "confidential from" — the information is hidden FROM someone.
알렉스|en|You said: They check the same person or not. Is it fake ID? So they confirm everything. It is wrong, better wrong.
민지|en|Natural: They check whether it's the same person and whether the ID is fake. They verify everything, which can take a long time.
알렉스|en|The word "verify" is very useful here. Verify your identity. Verify the document. It's professional and clear.
민지|en|Before we move on — a quick Pronunciation Spot! Two words gave Joey trouble this week.
알렉스|en|Word one: deductible. Let's break it down: de-DUCT-i-ble. The stress is on the second syllable — DUCT. Try it: de-DUCT-i-ble.
민지|en|Deductible. The key is that middle part — DUCT — like a duck. De-DUCK-tible!
알렉스|en|And word two: separately. se-pa-rate-ly. Four syllables. The tricky part is that third syllable — rate, not rit. se-pa-RATE-ly.
민지|en|Separately. One more time together: separately.
알렉스|en|These words sound intimidating, but once you break them into syllables, they're very manageable.
민지|ko|긴 단어는 음절로 쪼개면 훨씬 쉬워요. deductible은 de-DUCT-i-ble, separately는 se-pa-RATE-ly. 강세 위치를 기억하면 됩니다.
알렉스|en|Alright, let's move to Vocabulary in Context. These words came up this week. Let's make them real.
알렉스|en|Word one: deductible. This is the amount you pay yourself before insurance covers anything. Example sentence: My plan has a one-thousand dollar deductible, so I pay the first thousand myself.
민지|en|Think of a deductible as a threshold. You pay until you hit the number, and then insurance takes over.
알렉스|en|Word two: copay. A copay is a fixed fee for each visit. My copay for a doctor visit is thirty dollars. Same every time — simple to plan.
민지|ko|deductible은 연간 한 번 내는 기본 본인부담금이고, copay는 매번 병원 갈 때마다 내는 고정 금액이에요. 둘을 헷갈리지 마세요.
알렉스|en|Word three: out of pocket. This means you pay with your own money, not insurance. I had to pay five hundred dollars out of pocket for the surgery.
민지|en|Out of pocket usually implies it was expensive and unexpected. It carries a small sense of frustration.
알렉스|en|Word four: confidential. This is stronger than "secret" in professional contexts. The report is confidential. This information is strictly confidential.
민지|en|And word five: verify. To confirm or check that something is true or correct. Please verify your identity before proceeding.
알렉스|en|Let's also talk about collocations — words that naturally go together with these.
민지|en|For deductible: meet your deductible, hit your deductible, annual deductible. You meet or hit a deductible, like a target.
알렉스|en|For verify: verify your identity, verify the information, verify your account. Verify almost always goes with identity or information in business contexts.
민지|en|For confidential: strictly confidential, keep it confidential, treat it as confidential. Notice how we strengthen it with "strictly."
알렉스|en|Learning words in natural pairs like this is what separates intermediate English from advanced English.
민지|ko|단어 하나를 외우는 것보다, 함께 쓰이는 짝(연어)을 함께 외우면 훨씬 자연스럽게 쓸 수 있어요. "meet your deductible", "strictly confidential"처럼요.
알렉스|en|Now for Topic Talk — five everyday A2 to B1 expressions you can use right away.
민지|en|Expression one: sort something out. It means to solve or organize a problem. I need to sort out my insurance before the trip.
알렉스|en|Expression two: on top of it. This means you're handling something and it's under control. Don't worry about the forms — I'm on top of it.
민지|en|Expression three: bear with me. A polite way to ask for patience. Bear with me while I find the right form.
알렉스|en|Expression four: get the ball rolling. It means to start something moving forward. Let's get the ball rolling on that application.
민지|en|And expression five: bite the bullet. Wait — that's actually our Culture Corner story!
알렉스|en|Perfect timing. Culture Corner! This week's theme: bite the bullet.
민지|en|What does it mean?
알렉스|en|It means to do something difficult or painful with courage. I need to bite the bullet and get that tooth checked. I bit the bullet and paid the deductible.
민지|en|Where does the phrase come from?
알렉스|en|Before modern medicine, before anesthesia existed, battlefield surgeons had to operate on soldiers right away. No time, no drugs, no way to stop the pain.
민지|en|That sounds terrifying.
알렉스|en|It was. So surgeons gave soldiers a lead bullet to bite down on during the operation. The physical pressure helped them manage the pain.
민지|ko|마취제가 없던 시절, 전쟁터 수술에서 군인들이 고통을 참으려고 납 탄환을 입에 물었어요. 거기서 "bite the bullet" — 힘든 일을 용기 있게 버텨낸다는 표현이 생겼어요.
알렉스|en|And this week, Joey talked about health insurance, deductibles, and medical costs. Sometimes you really do have to bite the bullet and pay that deductible!
민지|en|Ha! Perfectly connected to this week. I love it when history meets real life.
알렉스|en|Also — this is a great example of an idiom that sounds extreme but is used casually. I bit the bullet and apologized. She bit the bullet and filed the taxes early.
민지|en|Ha! Perfectly connected to this week's topics. You really do have to bite the bullet and deal with all this paperwork.
알렉스|en|And once you do it in English? Even stronger confidence for next time.

알렉스|en|Steady is a foundation. The ceiling is higher — and fixing those top two patterns is how you break through it.
민지|en|Two focus points for next week. Number one: before every noun, ask yourself — first mention? Then a or an. Already known? Then the.
알렉스|en|Number two: when you see "every" or "each," the verb is always singular. Every country has. Each student is. And never drop the be verb from "it's."
