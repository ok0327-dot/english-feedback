알렉스|en|Subject-Verb Agreement. It has appeared eight times in your cumulative record. That puts it tied at number one.
민지|en|Eight times! So this is not a one-time thing. What exactly is the rule?
알렉스|en|In English, when the subject is "he," "she," or "it" — that's called third-person singular — the verb gets a small change. You add an "s" at the end.
민지|en|He speaks. She runs. It works. Sounds simple, right?
알렉스|en|It sounds simple, but here's why it's hard for Korean speakers. In Korean, verbs don't change based on who is doing the action. "나는 가요, 당신은 가요, 그는 가요" — the verb stays the same.
민지|ko|한국어는 주어가 바뀌어도 동사 형태가 그대로예요. 그래서 영어에서 he speaks의 -s를 깜빡하게 되는 거예요.
알렉스|en|Exactly. Your brain says "subject done, verb done." But English says "wait — if it's he, she, or it, you need to mark the verb too."
민지|en|It's like English wants to double-check the subject inside the verb itself.
알렉스|en|That's a great way to put it! And the cultural angle here is interesting. English grammar evolved from languages that marked verbs very carefully — Latin, Old English — where endings told you who was doing what even without a subject.
민지|ko|영어 동사에 -s를 붙이는 건 역사적 흔적이에요. 옛날엔 동사 끝만 봐도 주어를 알 수 있었거든요.
알렉스|en|So even though modern English dropped most of those endings, the third-person singular "s" survived. It's stubborn like that.
민지|en|I love that. Grammar as a historical survivor. OK, let's hear a real example from this week.
알렉스|en|Here's one. You said: "When I met some people, I feel awkward." Hear the mismatch? "met" is past tense, but "feel" jumps to present.
민지|en|Ah, so the tenses clash.
알렉스|en|Right. And the natural version: "When I meet new people, I sometimes feel awkward." Same tense throughout. And notice the word "sometimes" — that makes it feel like a real habit, not a universal fact.
민지|en|That one little word changes everything. "Sometimes" makes it sound so much more human.
알렉스|en|Native speakers add "sometimes," "usually," or "often" to soften statements about habits. It's more honest and more natural.
민지|ko|sometimes, usually, often 같은 부사를 습관 표현에 넣으면 훨씬 자연스럽고 진실되게 들려요.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: before you say the verb, check the subject. Is it "he," "she," or "it"? Then pause — and add that "s."
민지|en|He speaks. She runs. It works. Say it three times fast and make it a reflex!
알렉스|en|Let's move to our second deep-dive pattern — Articles. Also sitting at eight in your cumulative record.
민지|en|Articles. "A," "an," and "the." Why are these so hard, Alex?
알렉스|en|Because Korean simply does not have them. In Korean, you just say "사과" — apple. No "a" or "the" needed. So Korean learners often skip them entirely.
민지|ko|한국어에는 관사가 없어요. 그냥 '사과'라고 하면 되는데 영어는 'an apple'이냐 'the apple'이냐를 따져야 하죠.
알렉스|en|In English, "a" and "the" carry real information. "A" means: this is new, I'm introducing it for the first time, it's one of many. "The" means: we both already know which one I'm talking about.
민지|en|Oh, so "the" is like shared knowledge between the speaker and the listener?
알렉스|en|Exactly. Think of it as a signal you send to your listener. "I found a restaurant" — first time, new information. "The restaurant was amazing" — now we both know which one.
민지|en|It's like a little flag that says "you know this one."
알렉스|en|And here's the cultural contrast. English speakers use "the" very naturally because they assume the listener needs to be "brought in" to their mental world. In Korean conversation, context is often assumed shared, so you can skip that flag.
민지|ko|영어 화자는 'the'로 청자를 자신의 이야기 세계로 끌어들여요. 한국어는 맥락이 이미 공유됐다고 보고 그냥 말하는 경향이 있어요.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: first mention uses "a" or "an." After that, use "the." For general statements, use no article at all — just the bare noun.
민지|en|Let me try. "I like coffee." No article — general. "I bought a coffee this morning." One cup, first mention. "The coffee was too sweet." That specific one we're talking about.
알렉스|en|Perfect. You've got it!
민지|en|One more article tip before we move on. What about "an" versus "a"?
알렉스|en|Great question. Use "an" before a vowel sound — not just a vowel letter, but the sound. "An apple," "an hour" — "hour" starts with a vowel sound even though it's written with "h."
민지|en|So it's about how it sounds, not how it's spelled. "An honest person," "a university" — because "university" starts with a "y" sound.
알렉스|en|Exactly right. It's all about making the words flow easily in your mouth. English is very oral that way.

알렉스|en|The "-ing" form describes something that causes that emotion. A movie can be exciting. But you feel excited. If you feel it — use "-ed."

알렉스|en|Next. You said: "every place is possible to play the singing song."
민지|en|Better: "I can sing songs anywhere."
알렉스|en|Notice how the English version uses a real subject — "I." Korean often builds sentences around abstract ideas like "it is possible." English prefers a person as the subject. "I can" instead of "it is possible."
민지|en|Next. You said: "How about my opinion?"
알렉스|en|Better: "How about my suggestion?" The word "opinion" means what you think. "Suggestion" means something you want to offer. In this context, you're proposing something — so "suggestion" is the right word.
민지|en|Small word swap, completely different meaning. Good one.

민지|en|Better: "Summer is more suitable for outdoor activities." Again, flip the subject to "Summer" — make the sentence start with what you're really talking about.

민지|en|Better: "I'll go home and see my family."
알렉스|en|Two things here. First, "go home" — not "go back to my home." We usually just say "go home." Second, "see my family" — not "meet my family." You "meet" strangers. You "see" family. It feels warmer.
