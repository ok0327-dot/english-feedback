알렉스|en|And I'm Alex, your English coach. This is week 2026-W43. You did five lessons this week. That's fantastic.
민지|en|Five lessons! One for every day of the work week. Alex, what are we doing differently today?
알렉스|en|Today we're not just listing corrections. We're going to understand the why behind them.
민지|en|Because knowing why something sounds wrong helps you fix it faster than just memorizing the right version.
알렉스|en|When you understand the pattern, it sticks. Let's start with this week's number one story.
민지|en|Articles. A, an, the. Our all-time chart-topper with sixteen cumulative appearances in your data.
알렉스|en|Sixteen times. This is the hardest pattern for Korean speakers, and the reason is simple — Korean has no articles.
민지|ko|한국어에는 a, an, the 같은 관사가 없어요. 그래서 영어에서도 자연스럽게 건너뛰게 되는 거예요.
알렉스|en|So your brain just skips them. It's not laziness. It's your mother tongue working against you.
민지|en|So what do articles actually do? Give us the simple version, Alex.
알렉스|en|Think of articles as a signal. "A" says: this is new information. I'm introducing it for the first time.
민지|en|And "the" says: we both already know which one I mean. It's shared knowledge between speaker and listener.
알렉스|en|And no article at all means we're talking about something in general. Like "Dogs are friendly." Not any specific dog.
민지|ko|a는 처음 소개할 때, the는 둘 다 아는 것을 가리킬 때, 무관사는 일반적인 개념을 말할 때예요.
민지|en|Let's try a mini quiz. Which article goes in the blank? "I ordered __ coffee." First time you mention it.
알렉스|en|"A coffee." New information. You haven't told me which coffee yet.
민지|en|Good. Now later in the conversation: "__ coffee was delicious." We both know which coffee now.
알렉스|en|"The coffee." Shared knowledge. We're referring back to the specific one you ordered.
민지|en|One more: "Coffee is popular in Korea." No article. We're talking about coffee in general as a concept.
알렉스|en|Beautiful. Same word, three different articles depending on whether it's new, known, or general. That's the whole system.
알렉스|en|This week you said: "Yeah, it's a start, starting everything."
민지|en|And the natural version is: "It's the start of a new week."
알렉스|en|Why "the start"? Because it's this specific one. The start of this week. You and your teacher both know which one.
민지|en|Shared knowledge. So we use "the."
알렉스|en|Another example: "So usually the people gather and live in our apartment."
민지|en|Natural: "Usually people live in apartment complexes." No "the" before "people" — we mean people in general, not specific ones.
알렉스|en|And "the UK" — you said "from British, from the UK." That's actually almost right. "From the UK" is correct.
민지|en|Country names like Korea and Japan have no "the." But the United Kingdom is different.
알렉스|en|Because "United Kingdom" is a description, not just a name. Same for "the USA" and "the Philippines."
민지|en|Your rule of thumb: before any noun, ask — does my listener already know exactly which one I mean?
알렉스|en|Yes, they know? Use "the." No, it's new? Use "a." Is it a general idea? No article needed.
민지|en|Okay, our second deep-dive this week: Question Formation. Alex, why is this hard for Korean speakers?
알렉스|en|In Korean, you can change your intonation and any sentence becomes a question. "You went?" "You can come?"
민지|ko|한국어는 억양만 올리거나 어미만 바꿔도 질문이 돼요. 하지만 영어는 반드시 어순을 바꿔야 해요.
알렉스|en|In English, yes-no questions need a helper verb at the front. Do, does, did, is, are, can, will — these go first.
민지|en|You said this week: "Is the USA and Australia and United Kingdom and New Zealand gas station open have convenience stores?"
알렉스|en|I can feel the effort there. You put "Is" at the front, which is the right instinct. But the structure got tangled.
민지|en|The natural version: "Do gas stations in the USA, Australia, the UK, and New Zealand have convenience stores?"
알렉스|en|"Do gas stations have" — helper verb first, then subject, then main verb at the end.
민지|en|You also said: "And the cashier handle both gas payment and store purchase?"
알렉스|en|Better: "And do cashiers handle both gas payments and store purchases?" "Do" goes first.
민지|en|And my favorite from this week: "Is it possible to pre-talk?"
알렉스|en|Ha! I love the creativity of "pre-talk." But the natural phrase is: "Can we just chat informally first?"
민지|en|Rule of thumb for questions: flip the order. Instead of "You can help me?" say "Can you help me?" Helper verb first.
알렉스|en|Think of it like a light switch. Statement: subject on, verb follows. Question: flip it. Helper verb comes first.
민지|en|Great. Now let's run our Correction Clinic. Everyone, try to repeat the natural version after us.
알렉스|en|You said: "I don't distinguish the quality of the gasoline."
민지|en|Better: "I can't really tell the difference in gasoline quality." The phrase is "tell the difference," not just "distinguish."
알렉스|en|You said: "It makes it more easier."
민지|en|Better: "It makes it easier." You can't combine "more" with a word that already has "-er." Pick one.
알렉스|ko|'more easier'처럼 more와 -er을 같이 쓰는 건 이중 비교급이에요. 영어에서는 둘 중 하나만 써야 해요.
알렉스|en|You said: "It is only I have experience to rent a house in Korea."
민지|en|Better: "I've only had experience renting a house in Korea." The phrase is "have experience doing something."
알렉스|en|You said: "I like to do interior in some space."
민지|en|Better: "I enjoy doing interior design in various spaces." "Interior design" is the natural set phrase.
알렉스|en|You said: "because it will start autumn."
민지|en|Better: "because autumn is starting." Autumn is the subject here — it's the thing doing the starting.
알렉스|en|You said: "every tree has changed color, changed leaves."
민지|en|Better: "the leaves on every tree have changed color." Here "leaves" is the subject. They do the changing.
알렉스|en|You said: "This weekend, ah, this day we traveled to a small shop, small restaurant near my house."
민지|en|Better: "This past weekend, we went to a small shop and a small restaurant near my house."
알렉스|en|"This past weekend" makes it clearly in the past. "This weekend" can sometimes mean the upcoming one.
민지|en|And: "I guess he's the first time in this shopping center, a supermarket."
알렉스|en|Better: "I guess it's his first time at this supermarket." The subject is "it" — this experience — not "he."
민지|en|Two more quick ones. You said: "You were able to transfer your tickets or change it to something?"
알렉스|en|Natural: "Were you able to transfer or change your tickets?" The helper verb "were" goes to the front for the question.
민지|en|And: "And other things, just buy, just look around and check that we need this item."
알렉스|en|Natural: "For other things, we just look around and buy what we need." Clean subject, clear verb, direct object.
민지|en|These small structural tweaks make a big difference. Your ideas are clear — it's just the packaging.
알렉스|en|Exactly. The content is great. We're just polishing the English container it comes in.
민지|en|Excellent clinic! Now, Vocabulary in Context. Let's bring these words to life.
알렉스|en|First: "regulation." It means an official rule from an authority. Natural sentence: "Traffic regulations help keep pedestrians safe."
민지|en|"Pedestrian" — a person walking on a road. "Watch out for pedestrians at the crosswalk."
알렉스|en|"Enforce" — to make sure a rule is followed. "The city enforces parking regulations strictly."
민지|en|Notice the collocation: "enforce a regulation" or "enforce a law." You enforce rules — you don't "do" or "make" them work.
알렉스|en|"Distracted" — your attention is pulled away from what you're doing. "Don't drive while distracted."
민지|en|Very common in our smartphone age. "I got distracted by a notification."
알렉스|en|Housing vocabulary time. "Deposit" — money you pay before moving in, returned when you leave if there's no damage.
민지|en|"Lease agreement" — the legal contract between landlord and tenant. "Did you sign the lease agreement yet?"
알렉스|en|"Real estate agent" — the professional who helps you find property. In the US they're often called a "realtor."
민지|en|"Property" is the word for a building or piece of land. "She owns several rental properties."
알렉스|en|Topic Talk time! Five practical expressions from your week's topics.
민지|en|For travel: "My flight got delayed." Not "my flight was late" — "delayed" is the natural word for transport.
알렉스|en|For driving: "I'll top up the tank before we go." "Top up" means to add fuel until it's full.
민지|en|For shopping: "I'm just browsing, thank you." Perfect when a store assistant asks if you need help.
알렉스|en|For housing: "Is the apartment pet-friendly?" "Pet-friendly" means animals are allowed.
민지|en|And general small talk: "It's my first time here." Simple, natural, and opens conversations easily.

민지|en|Let's also look at "thrifty" and "impersonal" from this week's shopping topic.
알렉스|en|"Thrifty" means careful with money, spending as little as possible. "She's very thrifty — she always compares prices."
민지|en|It's a positive word, by the way. Being thrifty is a good quality. Different from "cheap," which can be negative.
알렉스|en|"Impersonal" means lacking personal warmth or connection. "The service felt very impersonal."
민지|en|Good contrast to remember: a small local shop feels personal. A big chain store can feel impersonal.
알렉스|en|And "streamlined" — made simpler and more efficient. "The new checkout process is more streamlined."
민지|en|Great words. Thrifty, impersonal, streamlined. Try to use one today.

알렉스|en|This week I want to talk about the word "deadline." You probably use this word every day.
민지|en|"The deadline is tomorrow." "I almost missed the deadline." It's everywhere.
알렉스|en|But do you know where it comes from? The word has a very dark origin.
민지|en|Tell me.
알렉스|en|It comes from the American Civil War in the 1860s. Military prisons had a physical line drawn on the ground around the prison walls.
민지|en|And if a prisoner crossed that line?
알렉스|en|Guards would shoot them. Dead line. Cross it and you are dead. That was the original deadline.
