민지|en|Four lessons this week: refund policies, Asian food markets, laundry and dry cleaning, and pharmacy visits.
알렉스|en|Four very different topics. But the grammar patterns hiding inside them are the same ones you keep building on.
민지|en|Today we don't just list the mistakes. We dig into why they happen and how to stop them for good.
알렉스|en|That's the whole point. Understanding the why. Let's go.
민지|en|Coming up: why articles keep disappearing, how to fix verb forms after try, real pharmacy vocabulary, and the surprisingly dark history of the word deadline.
알렉스|en|You won't think about deadlines the same way after this. Trust me.
민지|en|Section one: Why It Happens. Our number one pattern this week.
알렉스|en|And the biggest cumulative pattern over 94 lessons. Articles. A, an, the. Seventeen times in the records.
민지|en|Alex, be honest. Why does this one keep coming back even after so many lessons?
알렉스|en|Here is the honest answer. Korean does not have articles. Zero. Not one. There is no a, no an, no the in Korean grammar.
민지|ko|한국어에는 관사가 아예 없어요. 그래서 영어를 말할 때 뇌가 관사를 자동으로 건너뛰어 버립니다.
알렉스|en|So when you speak English your brain works hard to form sentences, and it quietly skips the article. Because in Korean it never needed one.
민지|en|And it doesn't feel wrong. Because in Korean it truly isn't wrong.
알렉스|en|But in English, articles carry real information. Let me show you with one example.
민지|en|Yes, please.
알렉스|en|"I saw a doctor" means some doctor, one of many, not your regular one. "I saw the doctor" means your doctor. The specific one we both already know about.
민지|en|Same words. Completely different meaning. The article does all that work.
알렉스|en|Now here's what we heard this week. You said: I don't have experience visit the Asian market abroad.
민지|en|The natural version: I haven't had the chance to visit an Asian market abroad.
알렉스|en|Hear the article? An Asian market. You're talking about Asian markets in general. First time you mention it. One of many possible markets. So you need a or an.
민지|ko|핵심 rule of thumb: 처음 말하는 가산명사 하나이면 무조건 a 또는 an. 이 한 줄만 기억하세요.
알렉스|en|Rule of thumb: If you introduce a countable noun for the first time, use a or an. Not the. Not nothing.
민지|en|One second before the noun. Ask yourself: first mention? Countable? One of many? If yes, a or an.
알렉스|en|More examples from daily life. "I need a phone charger." First mention, one of many. "Did you see the charger I left on the table?" That specific one we both know.
민지|en|I need a coffee right now. Not any specific coffee. Just one. And after you get it: where did the coffee go? That specific cup.
알렉스|en|Articles work like a pointer. A points forward. The points back at something already shared.
민지|en|Beautiful. Now our second big pattern this week: Verb Forms. Specifically after the verb try.
알렉스|en|Here's what we heard. You said: If you want something spicy, try put in gochujang.
민지|en|The natural version: If you want something spicy, try putting in gochujang.
알렉스|en|After try when you mean let's experiment and see what happens, you always use the -ing form. Try running. Try cooking. Try putting.
민지|ko|"try + 동작"은 항상 -ing형이에요. 실험의 의미일 때는 try doing, 목표를 위해 노력할 때는 try to do입니다.
알렉스|en|Here is the useful contrast. "Try to do something" means aiming for a goal, and it might be hard. "Try doing something" means let's experiment and see what happens.
민지|en|So: try to finish it before dinner - that's a goal. Try putting gochujang in - that's an experiment. Try adding more salt. Try calling again tomorrow.
알렉스|en|One more verb pattern from this week. You said: she wanted to need a refund.
민지|en|The natural version: she wanted a refund.
알렉스|en|Two verbs stacked where one is enough. Wanted to need. In English, one clear verb per action is the golden rule. Wanted a refund. Clean and done.
민지|en|Rule of thumb: if you can remove one verb and the sentence still makes perfect sense, remove it.
알렉스|en|Good. Now let's speed up with the Correction Clinic. Quick and clear. Repeat after us if you can.
민지|en|You said: Yeah, it's getting more colder.
알렉스|en|Better: Yeah, it's getting colder.
민지|en|Why? Colder already uses the -er comparative ending. Adding more on top is double comparison. Not allowed.
알렉스|en|Same rule: more bigger, more faster - not natural. Bigger, faster - correct. Pick one form and stick with it.
민지|en|You said: And when I prepare the present for my wife, the earring or some gold product, that product usually have a warranty.
알렉스|en|Better: When I buy presents for my wife, like earrings or other gold products, they usually come with a warranty.
민지|en|Two adjustments: prepare is for food, presentations, or plans. For gifts you buy them. And come with a warranty is the natural phrase.
알렉스|en|You said: usually if I travel to abroad, I just focus on their country.
민지|en|Better: Usually, when I travel abroad, I prefer to focus on experiencing the local culture.
알렉스|en|No to before abroad. The word abroad already means to a foreign country. Adding to is a redundancy. And their country is vague - local culture is more specific and natural.
민지|en|You said: But sometimes they instruction to the little weak.
알렉스|en|Better: But sometimes the instructions are a bit unclear.
민지|en|Instructions needs to be plural. And a bit unclear is a softer, more natural phrase.
알렉스|en|You said: Just to do many laundry. That is more easy for me anyway.
민지|en|Better: It's just easier for me to do a larger load of laundry.
알렉스|en|Laundry is uncountable in English. You can't say many laundry. You say a load of laundry or a lot of laundry. And more easy should be easier.
민지|en|You said: If any has a stain, I ask them to treat with a gentle detergent.
알렉스|en|Better: If anything has a stain, I ask them to treat it with a gentle detergent.
민지|en|Any alone cannot be the subject here. You need anything. And don't forget the pronoun it - treat it.
알렉스|en|You said: This day is awesome season.
민지|en|Better: It's an awesome season today. When we describe weather or time of year, we often start with it's. It's cold. It's a beautiful day. It's an awesome season.
알렉스|en|You said: So spring and autumn season is short in Korea. So, if we don't enjoy that season, it passes fast.
민지|en|Better: Spring and autumn are short in Korea. So, if we don't enjoy those seasons, they pass quickly.
알렉스|en|Three things: no article needed before spring and autumn as general concepts. Season becomes plural seasons to match. And passes fast becomes pass quickly - the verb agrees with the plural subject.
민지|en|You said: And I'm just middle-aged in life. Better: I'm middle-aged. The phrase in life adds nothing. Middle-aged tells the whole story already.
민지|en|You said: Because she works too late, so she wanted the non-drowsy medicine.
알렉스|en|Better: Because she will be working later, she needed the non-drowsy medicine.
민지|en|Will be working for a future plan. And needed in past tense since this was a story that already happened.
알렉스|en|Great job on the clinic. You heard a lot of patterns there. Two to remember: uncountable nouns like laundry, and one comparative ending is enough.
민지|en|Now let's go to Tutor's Desk.
알렉스|en|No live tutor notes for this week's sessions in our records. But the AI patterns we covered today are solid building blocks.
민지|en|Your live tutor and your AI feedback are working toward the same habits. They overlap more than you might expect.
알렉스|en|Now let's bring vocabulary to life. Not just definitions. Real English in action.
민지|en|First word: eligible. It means you qualify. You meet the conditions.
알렉스|en|Real sentences: This product is not eligible for a refund. Are you eligible for the student discount? You're not eligible to vote until you turn eighteen.
민지|en|Notice: eligible for something. Eligible to do something. The prepositions matter.
알렉스|en|Next: defective. Something defective has a flaw from the beginning. The screen is defective. The battery was defective when it arrived.
민지|en|Related noun: a defect. There's a defect in the product. Defect is the noun. Defective is the adjective.
알렉스|en|Malfunctioning. The printer kept malfunctioning during the meeting. It's working but badly. Not completely broken - just not right.
민지|en|Great for technology. My phone has been malfunctioning since the update. The app is malfunctioning again.
알렉스|en|Unresponsive. She called the pharmacy three times but they were completely unresponsive. No answer, no reaction, no help.
민지|en|Unresponsive works for people, machines, and websites. The page is unresponsive. The customer service team was unresponsive.
알렉스|en|And crave. I'm really craving something spicy right now. To crave means to want something strongly, almost desperately.
민지|en|You can crave food, you can crave attention, you can crave a long vacation. Strong desire - that's crave.
알렉스|en|One more: stain. There's a stain on your shirt. A stain is a mark or spot that is hard to remove. Coffee stain. Wine stain. Grass stain.
민지|en|From the laundry lesson: treat a stain. I need to treat this stain before it sets. Treat means to apply something to remove it. Act fast before it dries.
알렉스|en|And detergent. I always use a gentle detergent for my delicate clothes. Detergent is cleaning liquid or powder for laundry. Gentle detergent means soft - safe for sensitive or delicate fabrics.
알렉스|en|Now Topic Talk. New expressions connected to this week's themes.
민지|en|Pharmacy time. Three expressions you can use at any pharmacy in the world.
알렉스|en|First: over-the-counter. I picked up some cold medicine over-the-counter. No prescription needed. Walk in, find it on the shelf, pay, leave.
민지|en|Second: fill a prescription. I need to fill the prescription my doctor gave me. The doctor writes the order. The pharmacist fills it - prepares and gives you the medicine.
알렉스|en|Third: side effects. Always read the side effects before starting a new medicine. Drowsiness. Nausea. Headache. The unwanted things the medicine might do to your body.
민지|en|This week you talked about non-drowsy medicine. Good word: drowsy means feeling sleepy and slow. Non-drowsy means it won't make you tired.
알렉스|en|From the food market lessons: authentic. I love eating authentic Korean food when I travel. Not a copy. Not a tourist version. The real thing, made the traditional way.
민지|en|And locally sourced. This market only sells locally sourced vegetables. The ingredients came from nearby farms. Very natural and trendy expression now.
알렉스|en|One more for laundry day: dry-clean only. Be careful - this shirt is dry-clean only. You cannot wash it at home. It has to go to a professional dry cleaning shop.
민지|en|Now everyone's favourite segment. Culture Corner. Did You Know.
알렉스|en|This week's story: the word deadline.
민지|en|Deadline. We say it all the time. Work deadlines, school deadlines. But where does it actually come from?
알렉스|en|It comes from the American Civil War. The 1860s.
민지|en|The Civil War? That sounds very serious.
알렉스|en|It was. In prisoner-of-war camps, guards drew a physical line on the ground around the prison area. Any prisoner who stepped over that line would be shot immediately. They called it the dead line.
민지|ko|남북전쟁 포로수용소에서 넘으면 즉시 총살당하는 선을 'dead line'이라 불렀어요. 그게 오늘날 마감일이 됐습니다.
민지|en|That's very dark. And somehow this became the word we use for work projects?
알렉스|en|Later the word moved into newspaper printing. On old printing machines, there was a physical line marked on the flat metal printing plate. Any text past that line could not print. So editors had to finish before the dead line or lose the story.
민지|en|So the word traveled from you will be shot to your article cannot be printed to please send your report by Friday.
알렉스|en|Exactly. Language carries centuries of history inside it. Every ordinary word has a story you wouldn't expect.
